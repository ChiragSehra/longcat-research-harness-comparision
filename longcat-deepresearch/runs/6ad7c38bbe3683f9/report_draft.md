# Was India's 2024 general-election result a one-off correction or evidence of a durable political realignment

## S1｜Executive conclusion

### S1.1｜Decision and operational tests of durability

**The strongest supported answer is a consequential correction with institutional effects; durable national voter realignment remains unproved.** Confidence is moderate about the 2024 change, but low about its persistence through 11 October 2026 because comparable subsequent electoral and survey evidence was not recovered.

The decisive distinction is BJP versus NDA: BJP won 240 seats, below the 272 majority threshold, while NDA secured 293. Congress’s 99 seats likewise should not be conflated with INDIA’s broader strength. These results established dependence on partners at government formation without displacing BJP as the largest party. [PRS’s election profile](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha) corroborates party seats; [Vaishnav and Mallory’s September 2024 analysis](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/09/india-election-bjp-party-politics) supplies alliance totals and the strongest continuity argument: BJP remained the organizing pole of competition.

Governing evidence supports a qualified constraint interpretation. PRS records [Waqf legislation passing both chambers in April 2025](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-waqf-amendment-bill-2024), but [the constitutional delimitation bill being negatived on 17 April 2026](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-constitution-131st-amendment-bill-2026). This contrast suggests continuing legislative capacity alongside limits on constitutional change; it does not establish that allies caused the defeat.

Neither a *one-off* correction nor durable regional or voter-bloc realignment is demonstrated by the recovered evidence. Missing comparable returns and weighted survey findings prevent convergence tests. The mixed interpretation would strengthen if regional gains and bloc shifts recur alongside surviving alliances and documented bargaining concessions; broad electoral reversals would strengthen the correction case. These are discriminating tests, not forecasts.

## S2｜Electoral change and persistence

### S2.1｜National votes, seats, and electoral conversion

**The verified change is a substantial redistribution of parliamentary seats; the recovered evidence does not establish a comparably large national shift in popular support.** BJP fell from 303 seats in 2019 to 240 in 2024, while Congress rose from 52 to 99: calculated changes of −63 and +47. SP won 37 seats in 2024. These figures are corroborated by PRS’s ECI-based profiles; its 2019 profile covers 542 constituencies, explicitly excluding the postponed Vellore election. [2019 profile](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-newly-elected-17th-lok-sabha), [2024 profile](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha).

The following comparison supplies **secondary-source observations, not reconciled final ECI returns**. Votes are rounded to millions; “contested” reproduces the publisher’s constituency counts. No vote-share or contested-seat conversion calculation is made from these unreconciled inputs. [2014 data](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2014/), [2019 data](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2019/), [2024 data](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2024/).

| Party | 2014: votes / won / contested | 2019: votes / won / contested | 2024: votes / won / contested |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| BJP | 171.66m / 282 / 428 | 229.08m / 303 / 436 | 235.97m / 240 / 441 |
| Congress | 106.94m / 44 / 464 | 119.62m / 52 / 422 | 136.76m / 99 / 328 |

Taken provisionally, these observations are consistent with weaker BJP seat conversion despite more absolute votes, and stronger Congress conversion while contesting fewer constituencies. They do **not** establish coordination’s causal contribution: electorate growth, geographic distribution, candidate selection and changed contest patterns must be separated before attributing the result to vote transfer.

The regional-party arithmetic also requires care. IndiaVotes reports 2019→2024 seats of SP 5→37, AITC 22→29, TDP 3→16, JD(U) 16→12, YSRCP 22→4 and AAP 1→3. Its 2019 listing excludes Vellore, so DMK’s listed 23→22 cannot be treated as a complete-election comparison. Its 2024 categories distinguish Shiv Sena (7), Shiv Sena–UBT (9), and NCP–SP (8); PRS confirms that the latter two emerged from parties represented under different labels in 2019. Combining factions would conceal alliance redistribution. [2019 data](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2019/), [2024 data](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2024/), [PRS faction caveat](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha).

Denominator reconciliation remains essential. ECI’s **26 December 2024** release records 646,420,869 EVM-plus-postal votes, 6,371,839 NOTA votes (0.99%), and an uncontested Surat seat. Most statistical reports cover 542 polled constituencies, with specified supplementary tables including Surat. These totals differ from the secondary database’s aggregate; consequently, its published shares cannot substitute for a verified, consistently defined national series. [ECI release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2088064&reg=48&lang=2).

**Evidentiary limit:** final party/state/constituency tables, comparable turnout and vote-share denominators, complete BSP/BJD returns, and dated alliance classifications were not substantively recovered. Election-date NDA/UPA/INDIA totals, fixed-group comparisons and narrow-margin coordination estimates therefore remain unresolved. Earlier opposition alliances must not be retrospectively labelled INDIA. The defensible finding is a parliamentary correction; its magnitude in popular support and its durability remain unproven by this arithmetic alone.

### S2.2｜Regional redistribution and subsequent election tests

**The recovered regional evidence cannot establish a durable national realignment.** It contains indications of subsequent BJP recovery and contrasting regional outcomes, but lacks the final state-level parliamentary returns needed to rank contributions to the 2024 change. The distinction matters: BJP’s national total fell from 303 seats in 2019 to 240 in 2024, according to the fetched secondary election database; those totals alone cannot identify which regional mechanisms explain the decline. [IndiaVotes 2019](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2019/), [IndiaVotes 2024](https://www.indiavotes.com/lok-sabha/2024/).

Four regional clusters frame the available evidence, with differing verification strength:

- **Uttar Pradesh–Rajasthan:** the essential missing comparison. Substantive state returns were not recovered for either parliamentary election. Consequently, the evidence here cannot quantify BJP losses, compare vote and seat movements, or distinguish SP–Congress coordination from changing support for BJP or BSP. These mechanisms remain untested in this subsection; a ranked regional-loss calculation would be unsupported.

- **Haryana–Maharashtra:** provisional evidence against treating every 2024 setback as permanent. The fetched assembly database records BJP winning 48 Haryana seats and 132 Maharashtra seats in 2024. These are secondary party tallies, without recovered election-date alliance totals or comparable vote denominators. They indicate substantial subsequent electoral strength, but cannot establish reversal of the Lok Sabha verdict or measure Maharashtra’s faction-specific changes. [IndiaVotes assembly index](https://www.indiavotes.com/vidhan-sabha/).

- **Odisha–Andhra Pradesh:** concurrent comparisons, rather than subsequent persistence tests. ECI confirms that both assembly elections accompanied the 2024 parliamentary election and that their statistical reports were released on 26 December 2024; its microsite gives 4 June counting dates. The secondary database records BJP winning 78 Odisha assembly seats and TDP winning 135 Andhra Pradesh seats. This contrasts BJP-led expansion with a regional party’s victory, but the recovered evidence does not supply BJP/BJD parliamentary comparisons, the TDP–BJP–Jana Sena alliance total, or YSRCP vote changes. Aggregate results cannot demonstrate individual split-ticket voting. [ECI release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2088064&reg=48&lang=2), [ECI election microsite](https://elections24.eci.gov.in/), [IndiaVotes assembly index](https://www.indiavotes.com/vidhan-sabha/).

- **Later eastern and southern tests:** reported outcomes challenge a uniform opposition-realignment interpretation, but exact figures require reconciliation. A fetched May 2026 report describes BJP victory in West Bengal, continued strength in Assam, and TVK emerging as Tamil Nadu’s largest party. However, that page contradicts itself about the Kolathur winner, while fetched sources disagree on Bengal and Tamil Nadu seat totals. Those figures should therefore be excluded from decisive calculations. [Indian Express, 6 May 2026](https://indianexpress.com/article/india/election-commission-of-india-eci-tamil-nadu-assam-puducherry-west-bengal-kerala-election-results-2026-live-eciresults-nic-in-results-eci-gov-in-10669077/).

**Coverage remains incomplete through 11 October 2026.** ECI verifies the four concurrent assemblies, including Arunachal Pradesh and Sikkim, but the subsequent census lacks recovered direct declarations, alliance classifications, and comparable denominators. Secondary listings include the candidate 2024–2026 elections, yet cannot certify census completeness or exclude additional elections. Bihar, Karnataka, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal and the Northeast also lack recovered parliamentary comparisons sufficient for the required contribution screen. [ECI statistical-release description](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2088064&reg=48&lang=2), [IndiaVotes assembly index](https://www.indiavotes.com/vidhan-sabha/).

The defensible interpretation is therefore limited: available observations are compatible with regional volatility and BJP recovery; they do not demonstrate enduring voter switching. Confidence in a comprehensive regional adjudication must remain low until the missing returns and conflicting later reports are resolved.

### S2.3｜Major voter blocs and explanations of vote choice

**The verified survey coverage does not, by itself, establish durable voter-bloc realignment.** Lokniti-CSDS lists unweighted post-poll samples of 22,295 respondents across 26 states or Union Territories and 306 constituencies in 2014; 24,236 across 26 and 208 in 2019; and 19,663 across 23 and 191 in 2024. Its catalogue distinguishes these samples from weighted reports and lists questionnaires and methodological notes. These figures verify the reported coverage, but also show that geographic coverage differs between elections. [Lokniti national-election-study catalogue](https://www.lokniti.org/national-election-studies)

The underlying weighted tables, instruments and methodological notes remain unresolved in this evidence set. Consequently, no defensible numerical comparison can yet be supplied for SC/Dalit, ST/Adivasi, OBC, upper-caste, Muslim, female, young, poorer or rural voters. Uttar Pradesh’s Yadav/non-Yadav OBC and Jatav/other Dalit contrasts likewise remain unverified. National sample totals cannot establish precision for these subgroups; comparisons require subgroup denominators, weighting details, geographic coverage and consistent category definitions. BSP’s electoral decline alone cannot identify which caste groups switched parties.

**Voting support and political inclusion also require separate judgments.** In the published abstract of his April 2025 essay, Feyaad Allie argues that the 2024 result reinforced Muslim exclusion through BJP exclusion and opposition “strategic silence.” This is an attributed interpretation about inclusion, rather than a verified estimate of Muslim vote choice; the full essay was not recovered. [Allie, *India’s New Minority Politics*](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/indias-new-minority-politics/)

Reported concerns about jobs, prices, farming, welfare, leadership, reservation and constitutional protection require the original questions and response tables before they can support explanations of vote choice. Neither campaign promises nor economic indicators establish voting motives. No later comparable subgroup study is verified here, so persistence through 11 October 2026 remains unresolved. The appropriate judgment is **insufficient verified evidence**, rather than either continuity or realignment: even comparable repeated cross-sections would establish changes in group-level support, not track individual voters’ switching.

## S3｜Coalition organization and governing constraints

### S3.1｜Seat-sharing, alliance survival, and bargaining linkage

**The verified evidence establishes a need for parliamentary partners after 2024, but does not establish durable alliance cohesion or the extent of allies’ policy leverage.** BJP won 240 of 543 seats, compared with Congress’s 99 and SP’s 37. Researcher calculation: BJP was 32 seats short of a majority of the fully constituted House. That demonstrates dependence on additional support; it does not establish that any particular ally could individually bring down the government. Formation support letters and a dated, vacancy-adjusted membership record for 11 October 2026 remain unverified, preventing a reliable comparison of dependence at formation and cutoff. [PRS, *Profile of the 18th Lok Sabha*](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha).

**Election coordination and organizational survival require separate judgments.** The available evidence does not substantively recover the dated SP–Congress, Congress–AAP, DMK–Congress, RJD-led Bihar, or Maharashtra seat-sharing agreements, nor the TDP–BJP–Jana Sena and BJP–JD(U) arrangements. Consequently, their allocations, candidate exceptions and subsequent survival cannot be presented here as verified findings. TMC’s relationship with INDIA likewise requires distinguishing national cooperation from state electoral competition. Maharashtra comparisons must preserve the separate Sena and NCP factions rather than treating them as unchanged parties; PRS explicitly identifies the faction discontinuities in its election comparison. No measured vote-transfer finding from S2.1 is available in this record. [PRS](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha).

**Fiscal assistance is evidence of an announcement, not sufficient evidence of bargaining causation.** The 23 July 2024 Budget announced ₹15,000 crore in financial support for Andhra Pradesh’s new capital. It also proposed a development plan covering Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha and Andhra Pradesh—an alternative explanation broader than rewarding two allies. [PRS, *Union Budget 2024–25 Analysis*](https://prsindia.org/budgets/parliament/union-budget-2024-25-analysis). Without recovered, dated demands from N. Chandrababu Naidu or Nitish Kumar and evidence connecting those demands to the decision, attributing assistance to TDP or JD(U) leverage remains unproven. Announcements, releases and expenditure must be assessed separately in S3.2. The supported interpretation is therefore narrower than durable realignment: **2024 changed BJP’s parliamentary position; persistent coalition bargaining and repeatable opposition cooperation remain insufficiently verified through the cutoff.**

### S3.2｜Legislative control, constitutional arithmetic, and administrative adaptation

**The evidence supports continued ordinary legislative control alongside a consequential constitutional constraint; it does not establish that allies routinely dictated policy.** The comparison is mixed: PRS recorded 179 non-financial bills passed during the 17th Lok Sabha and 16% referred to committees; by Monsoon 2026, the committee-referral proportion in the 18th Lok Sabha was 21%. Increased scrutiny therefore coexisted with continued executive command of legislative business. These are PRS compilations, rather than independently recovered parliamentary records. ([17th Lok Sabha assessment](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/functioning-of-the-17th-lok-sabha), [Monsoon 2026 assessment](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/functioning-of-parliament-during-monsoon-session-2026))

**Ordinary legislation—Waqf:** introduced on 8 August 2024 and referred to a joint committee, the Waqf amendment subsequently passed Lok Sabha on 2 April and Rajya Sabha on 3 April 2025, according to PRS’s legislative chronology. Both chambers debated it for approximately thirteen hours. Referral delayed and scrutinised the proposal; it did not prevent passage. This is strong contrary evidence to an interpretation of 2024 as creating general opposition veto power, although passage alone cannot establish freedom from negotiations over the final text. ([Bill chronology](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-waqf-amendment-bill-2024), [Budget Session 2025 assessment](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/parliament-functioning-in-budget-session-2025))

**Constitutional change—delimitation:** PRS records introduction of the three-bill package on 16 April 2026 and negation of the Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill on 17 April. The proposals would have increased Lok Sabha’s constitutional maximum to 850, enabled delimitation using the latest published census—then 2011—and removed the requirement tying women’s reservation to the first census after the 2023 amendment. These were proposals, not enacted redistribution. PRS reports 298 votes for and 230 against; the associated ordinary bills consequently became infructuous and were not considered. The result demonstrates a constitutional barrier despite a numerical majority voting in favour. Without the official division list and contemporaneous party positions, it cannot establish ally defection or identify coalition bargaining as the cause. ([Package analysis](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-constitution-131st-amendment-bill-2026), [Budget Session 2026 assessment](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/parliament-functioning-in-budget-session-2026))

The strongest continuity counterexample comes later: PRS reports eleven bills passed during Monsoon 2026, nine without any Lok Sabha member other than the minister speaking. Conversely, the simultaneous-election constitutional proposal remained listed at the committee stage; referral itself supplies no evidence of defeat. ([Monsoon 2026 assessment](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/functioning-of-parliament-during-monsoon-session-2026), [Simultaneous-election bill](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-constitution-one-hundred-and-twenty-ninth-amendment-bill-2024))

**Administrative and fiscal attribution remains unresolved.** PRS records Cabinet approval of caste enumeration in the upcoming census in April 2025, but that decision alone cannot distinguish ally pressure from broader electoral adaptation. The lateral-recruitment cancellation documents, official constitutional proceedings, and substantive budget-implementation reports were not recovered. Consequently, this section cannot verify Andhra Pradesh/Bihar releases or expenditure, or count announced commitments as delivered bargaining concessions. Those gaps limit causal confidence without reversing the narrower finding of legislative continuity and selective constitutional constraint. ([April 2025 policy review](https://prsindia.org/policy/monthly-policy-review/april-2025))

## S4｜Competing interpretations and adjudication

### S4.1｜Strongest correction case, strongest realignment case, and mixed verdict

**The strongest correction case is continuity of BJP primacy alongside reduced autonomy.** In June 2024, BJP remained the largest parliamentary party with 240 seats, against Congress’s 99 and SP’s 37. This supports continued predominance, although seats alone cannot establish continuity in popular support. Its vulnerability is equally concrete: 240 seats did not constitute a majority of the 543-seat House. Calling the result merely a correction understates the change in the parliamentary conditions for governing. [PRS, *Profile of the 18th Lok Sabha*](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/profile-of-the-18th-lok-sabha).

Subsequent governing conduct strengthens the continuity argument. The Waqf amendment passed both Houses in April 2025. PRS reports that Parliament passed eleven bills during the July–August 2026 Monsoon Session, nine without any non-minister speaking in Lok Sabha. These observations demonstrate continued legislative capacity; they do not establish that allies exercised no influence over policy. [Waqf bill chronology](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-waqf-amendment-bill-2024); [PRS, Monsoon Session 2026](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/functioning-of-parliament-during-monsoon-session-2026).

**The strongest realignment case concerns governing constraints, rather than demonstrated permanent voter switching.** The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill was introduced on 16 April 2026 and negatived the following day. Read alongside ordinary-law passage, this supports a differentiated interpretation: a government can retain agenda control while encountering consequential limits on constitutional change. Its vulnerability is attribution. The outcome alone cannot establish ally rebellion, explain the decisive votes, or prove that the constraint resulted specifically from 2024. [PRS, delimitation package](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-constitution-131st-amendment-bill-2026).

Milan Vaishnav’s July 2024 essay offers a distinct continuity hypothesis: coalition dependence can coexist with persistence of the political order he calls India’s “Second Republic.” The accessible summary identifies changes in electoral democracy, constitutionalism, national identity, secularism and federalism. This is an attributed interpretation, not an established description. The full essay remains unavailable in the supplied evidence, so its detailed reasoning cannot be assessed; neither can substantive disagreements with Sanjay Kumar, Suhas Palshikar, Rahul Verma or Christophe Jaffrelot be responsibly reconstructed. [Vaishnav, publication and summary](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/the-rise-of-indias-second-republic/).

**The defensible mixed verdict is institutional change within continuing BJP predominance.** Temporary correction remains plausible but unproven; persistent voter-bloc realignment and durable regional party-system change remain unresolved without the comparable electoral and survey findings. Parliamentary constraint has stronger observational support, but enduring ally leverage requires documented bargaining and repeated outcomes. Ordinary-law passage and constitutional defeat distinguish institutional mechanisms; neither, by itself, resolves whether voters have durably changed allegiance.

## S5｜Uncertainties and limits

### S5.1｜Unresolved evidence and discriminating future tests

**Uncertainties and limits.** The evidence recovered for this subsection does not establish a complete election census through 11 October 2026. The [ECI elections page](https://www.eci.gov.in/elections) and [statistical-reports page](https://www.eci.gov.in/statistical-reports) returned no substantive records; the [results portal](https://results.eci.gov.in/) returned only a redirect. Completed state elections therefore cannot be inventoried reliably here. This is a retrieval gap, not evidence that elections did not occur. Without the other units’ verified manifests, comprehensive persistence claims remain unassessable; schedules cannot substitute for declared results.

Survey coverage also changes: Lokniti lists **22,295 respondents across 26 states/UTs and 306 constituencies in 2014; 24,236/26/208 in 2019; and 19,663/23/191 in 2024**. These are unweighted sample counts, not subgroup precision estimates. Weighting documentation, questionnaires and relevant later state reports were not recovered here, preventing a full comparability assessment. ([Lokniti NES](https://www.lokniti.org/national-election-studies)) Self-reported voting requires allowance for reporting error; subgroup switching claims require sample sizes and uncertainty estimates. Repeated cross-sections cannot identify individual switchers, and constituency changes cannot identify caste-level movement.

Alliance comparisons require election-date membership, consistent vote denominators, faction identifiers and seats contested. Assembly outcomes test regional persistence but cannot directly establish national realignment. Governing attribution similarly requires dated demands, decisions and delivery: a concession alone does not identify ally bargaining as its cause. Parliamentary proceedings and implementation records were not recovered here. No specific integrity complaint, official response or adjudicated finding was recovered that could support an integrity-based adjustment.

Three observable tests could change the conclusion: another national election reproducing the regional pattern; comparable surveys showing sustained bloc shifts; and alliance survival accompanied by documented, repeated bargaining concessions. Until verified, these are discriminators, not forecasts.
